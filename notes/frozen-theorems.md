# The model and its conventions

Pyrochlore spin ice states are the two-in--two-out configurations of Ising spins
on the pyrochlore lattice [@bramwell-gingras-2001-spin-ice-state], equivalently the Eulerian orientations
of the diamond lattice whose bonds carry the spins and whose sites are the
tetrahedron centres. Everything below uses that second picture and the
conventions of `icemix/lattice.py`.

Positions are integers in units of $a/4$, a quarter of the conventional cubic
edge. The A sublattice is generated by the face-centred cosets $(0,0,0)$,
$(0,2,2)$, $(2,0,2)$ and $(2,2,0)$, and the B sublattice is that set translated
by $(1,1,1)$. The four displacements from an A site to its B neighbours are
$d_0 = (1,1,1)$, $d_1 = (1,-1,-1)$, $d_2 = (-1,1,-1)$ and $d_3 = (-1,-1,1)$,
which sum to zero. An $(a,b,c)$ block of conventional cubic cells with periodic
boundaries has box $(4a,4b,4c)$ and holds $8abc$ sites and $16abc$ bonds. A state
assigns one bit to each bond, and the bit is $1$ exactly when the arrow runs from
the A end to the B end. The ice rule is that exactly two of the four arrows at
each site point in, which in this encoding reduces at both sublattices to
'exactly two of the four incident bits are set'. An ice state's arrow field is
divergence-free, so the net flux through each coordinate cut plane is conserved
by any ice-preserving move; that triple is the winding
$\mathbf{W} = (W_x, W_y, W_z)$, and $\mathbf{W} = 0$ is the largest sector and
the one at issue here.

Hexagons are derived rather than hardcoded, as the closed non-backtracking
six-walks whose unwrapped vector displacement is exactly zero. The displacement
condition is not pedantry: on a small periodic cell the quotient graph carries
six-cycles that wind round the torus instead of bounding a face, and flipping one
changes the winding. At $(1,1,1)$ the quotient graph is $K_{4,4}$, which has 96
six-cycles where the lattice has 16 hexagons. Each hexagon visits three A sites
and three B sites, so its six bonds fall into a class of three traversed from A
to B and a class of three traversed from B to A, alternating in cycle order.
There are $16abc$ hexagons, six through every bond and twelve through every A
site.

A hexagon is flippable when its arrows run head to tail, which says that one
traversal class carries all ones and the other all zeros. The chain picks a
hexagon uniformly at random and reverses it with probability $1/2$ if it is
flippable. Every flip preserves the ice rule and the winding, and the chain is
reversible with the uniform stationary distribution on each flux sector; its
generator is, up to normalisation, the ring-exchange Hamiltonian of pyrochlore
spin ice at its Rokhsar--Kivelson point [@rokhsar-kivelson-1988-superconductivity-quantum-hard-core-dimer-gas; @henley-2004-classical-to-quantum-dynamics-at-rohsar-kivelson-points;
@hermele-et-al-2004-pyrochlore-photons-u1-spin-liquid]. A state with no flippable hexagon is **frozen**: absorbing and
unreachable at once, a singleton component of the flip graph, and an exact
zero-energy eigenstate of that Hamiltonian, degenerate with the equal-weight
superposition over its sector. Whether frozen states exist at every size is
therefore a question about the ground-space degeneracy.

# The smallest cell is frozen

The single conventional cubic cell $(1,1,1)$ has sixteen bonds, eight sites and
sixteen hexagons. Write a state as a $4\times4$ zero-one matrix $M[g][k]$ whose
rows are the four A-site cosets and whose columns are the four directions, and
label the B end of the bond $(g,k)$ by $g \oplus k$; the labelling is exact
because the differences of the four directions realise the Klein four-group on
the cosets. The ice rule at the A sites is then 'row sums are 2', the ice rule at
the B sites is 'B-label sums are 2', and $\mathbf{W} = 0$ is 'column sums are 2'.
The first two conditions alone admit the classical 90 matrices with row and
column sums two, the $K_{4,4}$ count; all three admit exactly 12.

**Theorem 1.** *Every $\mathbf{W} = 0$ ice state of the $(1,1,1)$ cell is
frozen.*

*Proof.* A hexagon runs through the A sites $g$, $A_2 = g \oplus i \oplus j$ and
$A_3 = g \oplus j \oplus k$ for a triple $(i,j,k)$ of distinct directions, and
carries two bonds of each of those directions, one in each traversal class.
Head-to-tail circulation forces the two bonds of each direction to carry opposite
bits. Let $l$ be the fourth direction and $A_4 = g \oplus i \oplus k$ the fourth A
site, and take the three matrix cells $(A_3,k)$, $(A_3,l)$ and $(A_4,k)$, none of
which lies on the hexagon. They pairwise share a line: the first two share the
row $A_3$, the first and third share the column $k$, and the last two share the
B-label $g \oplus i$, because $A_4 \oplus k = A_3 \oplus l = g \oplus i$. Each of
those lines already carries two hexagon cells, one from each traversal class and
so of opposite bit, namely $(A_3,j)$ and $(A_3,i)$ in the row, $(A_2,k)$ and
$(g,k)$ in the column, and $(g,i)$ and $(A_2,j)$ in the B-label. Each line sums
to two, by the A-site ice rule, zero flux and the B-site ice rule respectively,
so its two remaining cells sum to one. The three off-hexagon cells would then
pairwise sum to one, which three zero-one numbers cannot do. $\square$

Periodic tiling lifts this to arbitrary size. Identify bonds of a large cell by
their A-site position reduced modulo the small box together with their direction,
and repeat a state of the small cell across the large one. The ice rule is local
and so is inherited; each winding component is multiplied by the tiling factor in
the two transverse directions, so a zero-flux state tiles to a zero-flux state;
and a hexagon of the large cell is a translate of a hexagon of the small one, so
frozenness is inherited too. That was checked from scratch in each target cell's
own derived hexagon set for ten source-target pairs, with targets $(2,2,2)$,
$(2,2,4)$, $(1,2,4)$ and the 432-spin $(3,3,3)$, all ten frozen with the ice rule
and zero winding intact. The $\mathbf{W} = 0$ sector of every cell therefore
contains frozen states.

# Translation-frozen states, and how many there are

The second construction is independent of the first and carries an exact count.
For the $z$ axis the relevant translations are the two half-diagonals
perpendicular to it, $(2,2,0)$ and $(2,-2,0)$ in $a/4$ units; the $x$ and $y$
axes take $(0,2,2)$, $(0,2,-2)$ and $(2,0,2)$, $(2,0,-2)$.

**Theorem 2.** *In any cell, an ice state invariant under the two half-diagonal
translations perpendicular to an axis is frozen, in every flux sector. Its
$\mathbf{W} = 0$ members number exactly $\binom{2c}{c}^{2}$, where $c$ is the
cell's extent along that axis, and the three axis families are disjoint.*

*Proof.* On a hexagon with directions $(i,j,k)$ and A sites $A_1, A_2, A_3$, the
two bonds of direction $i$ sit at $A_1$ and $A_3 = A_1 + (d_k - d_j)$, those of
direction $j$ at $A_3$ and $A_2 = A_3 + (d_i - d_k)$, and those of direction $k$
at $A_2$ and $A_1 = A_2 + (d_j - d_i)$, so each direction's pair is separated by
the difference of the other two. The four directions split by the sign of their
$z$ component into $\{d_0, d_3\}$ and $\{d_1, d_2\}$, so any three of them
contain a pair from one class, and for such a pair the difference has zero $z$
component and equals $\pm(d_0 - d_3) = \pm(2,2,0)$ or
$\pm(d_1 - d_2) = \pm(2,-2,0)$. One of the three directions therefore has its two
bonds identified by a translation the state is invariant under, so they carry
equal bits, where flippability requires them opposite. No hexagon is flippable,
and neither the ice rule nor any flux condition entered the argument.

For the count, quotient by the two translations. The A sites collapse to the $2c$
distinct coordinates along the axis and the quotient is a ring of $4c$ sites
joined by double bonds. Write $a(z)$ for the number of A-to-B arrows among the
two directions with positive axis component in layer $z$, and $b(z)$ for the same
among the two with negative component. The A-site rule gives $a(z) + b(z) = 2$
and the B-site rule gives $a(z) + b(z{+}2) = 2$, which together force only that
$b$ is 2-periodic; the fully polarised state satisfies both and has
$a \equiv 2$. It is zero flux along the axis, not the ice rule, that forces one
arrow up and one down on every double bond. Given that balance, the state is
fixed by one sign per layer for the upward pair and one for the downward pair,
and the two transverse fluxes vanish exactly when each of those two sequences of
length $2c$ is balanced. There are $\binom{2c}{c}$ balanced sequences of each
kind. A state in two of the three axis families is invariant under the whole
face-centred lattice, hence a $q = 0$ state, which has $\mathbf{W} \neq 0$, so
the families are disjoint at zero flux. $\square$

The corollary is a lower bound: an $L \times L \times L$ cell holds at least
$3\binom{2L}{L}^{2}$ frozen $\mathbf{W} = 0$ states, which grows like $16^{L}$ up
to a polynomial factor. The frozen set is exponentially large, and still
negligible against a sector whose size grows like $e^{\kappa L^{3}}$.

Both halves are checked numerically. The family is constructed and every member
verified for the ice rule, zero flux and zero flippable hexagons at $(1,1,2)$,
$(1,2,2)$, $(2,2,2)$ and $(3,3,3)$, the three axis families disjoint at each; and
where the whole frozen set is available by enumeration the number of
$(2,2,0)$-invariant frozen states is exactly $\binom{2c}{c}^{2}$, namely 36 at
$(1,1,2)$, 400 at $(1,1,3)$ and 4,900 at $(1,1,4)$, the last by SAT
enumeration (`experiments/07_certificates/results/translation_family.json`,
which also records 5,412 frozen zero-flux states in all at $(1,1,4)$). Neither theorem exhausts the frozen set: only four of the
twelve $(1,1,1)$ states belong to Theorem 2's family, and frozen states with no
translation symmetry at all exist on the thin cells.

Two features of the $(2,2,2)$ row are worth separating. At the smallest cubic
cell 192 of the 612 frozen zero-flux states, a little under a third, are neither
members of Theorem 2's family nor tilings of anything smaller, so the two
theorems and tiling together still miss a substantial part of the frozen set.
And every frozen zero-flux state of $(2,2,2)$ is periodic: the stabiliser-order
histogram under the 32 face-centred translations is
$\{2\!:\!192,\ 4\!:\!288,\ 8\!:\!120,\ 16\!:\!12\}$, minimum order two. That is a
qualitative difference from the thin cells, where aperiodic frozen states are
common, and it is a fact about $L = 2$ rather than about cubic cells in general.

# What the frozen set does to the dynamics

Because frozen states exist in the $\mathbf{W} = 0$ sector of every cell, that
sector is never connected under hexagon flips and its spectral gap is exactly
zero at every size. Every statement about relaxation, mixing or the
Rokhsar--Kivelson gap must therefore attach to a connected component of the flip
graph rather than to the sector, and any future rapid-mixing theorem must carry
the same restriction. The natural component is the one containing the chain's
start state, and it should not be assumed to be the largest: at $(1,1,4)$ the
canonical start sits in a component of 42,240 states while the giant component
has 43,008, and reading the gap off the wrong one misstates it by a factor of
3.6.

# Certification

Each theorem is checked by machine in two independent ways, and the counts above
in a third. The three artefacts answer different questions and have different
trust boundaries.

## The Lean 4 developments

Both theorems are formalised in Lean 4 [@de-moura-ullrich-2021-lean4-theorem-prover], each as a standalone Lake
project with no dependencies: core Lean only, no Mathlib, no network fetch,
toolchain pinned to `leanprover/lean4:v4.33.1`.

`formal/theorem1/` proves Theorem 1 about the $4\times4$ matrix model. The
structured proof follows the argument above, with the three line lemmas as
separate named theorems and the contradiction as one `omega` step over their
equations; an independent brute force over all $2^{16}$ matrices proves the same
statement, evaluated by the Lean kernel through `decide +kernel` rather than by
the compiler. The counts 90 and 12 are theorems rather than evaluations: for each
list of states there is a length, a soundness, a distinctness and a completeness
theorem quantified over all matrices, which together constitute a cardinality in
a setting with no `Fintype` or `Finset`.

`formal/theorem2/` proves Theorem 2 on the infinite lattice, in a form stronger
than the statement above in four respects. The site set is all of $\mathbb{Z}^3$
rather than the face-centred sublattice; there is no periodic cell and no modular
arithmetic; no ice rule is assumed, which is why 'in every flux sector' costs
nothing; and all three axes come from one lemma parameterised by the axis.
Sharpness is formalised, by a state invariant under one of the two translations
with a flippable hexagon. The translation-frozen family is formalised as far as
core Lean allows: invariance, the ice rule at both sublattices, freezing,
faithfulness of the parameterisation by two sign sequences, and the converse that
invariance forces layer-constancy and that layer-constancy with ice and balance
forces the two-sign-sequence form. The count $\binom{2c}{c}^{2}$ is *not*
formalised and cannot be without Mathlib, since core Lean has neither
`Nat.choose` nor `Finset`; nor are the torus quotient, the flux argument or the
disjointness corollary. It was the formalisation that caught an error in an
earlier derivation of the count, the claim that the ice rule alone forces one
arrow up and one down on each double bond; the counterexample is formalised as
`allUp`.

The gap between the formal model and the simulation is closed in both directions.
`Theorem2/Walk.lean` defines a hexagon as the simulation does and proves by
exhaustion over all $4^6 = 4096$ direction sequences that the closed
non-backtracking six-walks of zero displacement are exactly the recipe hexagons
$(i,j,k,i,j,k)$ with $i$, $j$, $k$ distinct; it identifies the two traversal
classes as lists in cycle order and restates Theorem 2 for walks.
`Theorem2/Count.lean` proves the incidence counts, twelve hexagons through every
A site and six through every bond, for every point of $\mathbb{Z}^3$.
`Export.lean` reverses the direction of trust: `lake exe hexexport` computes the
hexagon table of a periodic cell from the Lean definitions and nothing else, and
`tests/test_lean_bridge.py` checks the simulation against those tables at
$(1,1,1)$, $(1,1,2)$, $(1,2,2)$ and $(2,2,2)$, comparing the hexagon sets, the
cycle order up to rotation and reflection, the traversal partition, and the
flippability verdict against the reference predicate on all 90 ice states of
$(1,1,1)$.

The axiom audits are the reason to call these machine-checked rather than
machine-assisted. `formal/theorem1/Axioms.lean` and `formal/theorem2/Check.lean`
print `#print axioms` for 20 and 78 theorems respectively, and every line shows
only `propext`, `Quot.sound`, or no axioms at all. `Classical.choice` appears
nowhere, so both developments are constructive; `sorryAx` appears nowhere, so
nothing is admitted; and `Lean.ofReduceBool` appears nowhere, so `native_decide`
is unused and every finite check is carried out by the kernel. What remains
unverified is one transcription: some thirty lines of Lean definitions, covering
the coordinates, directions, hexagon sites, traversal classes, flippability and
the exporter's box and wrapping, have to be read against the conventions of
`icemix/lattice.py`, and if one of those is wrong both sides are wrong together.
The exporter is also a program rather than a proof, built from the same
definitions as `Count.lean` but not verified against them.

## Unsatisfiability certificates

The second check re-derives each theorem's finite instances by a route sharing no
code with the first. `icemix/cnf.py` encodes the model in conjunctive normal form
with the arrow bits as the Boolean variables. The ice rule is 'exactly two of
four' at every site, eight clauses with no auxiliaries. Zero flux uses one cut
plane per axis, which suffices because flux is conserved, and $W_j = 0$ becomes a
single cardinality constraint; that this cut-plane flux equals the project's
winding function bit for bit is checked over every ice state at three cells
rather than assumed. Flippability is 'the six bits alternate in cycle order', so
'frozen' is two six-clauses per hexagon and 'some hexagon is flippable' is a
selector per hexagon plus one clause. Translation invariance is two clauses per
bond.

Each theorem becomes an unsatisfiability claim. For Theorem 1 the formula is 'ice
rule, and $\mathbf{W} = 0$, and some hexagon is flippable' at $(1,1,1)$; for
Theorem 2 it is 'ice rule, and invariant under both half-diagonals perpendicular
to the axis, and some hexagon is flippable', with no zero-flux constraint at all,
which is what makes the certificate establish the theorem in its 'every flux
sector' form. CaDiCaL rel-3.0.1 refutes each and writes a DRAT proof, and
`drat-trim` [@wetzler-et-al-2014-drat-trim] checks it and must print `s VERIFIED`. The guarantee
is proof-theoretic rather than a claim about the solver: the proof is a sequence
of clause additions, each independently confirmed by the checker to preserve
satisfiability, ending in the empty clause. The solver's reasoning, heuristics
and correctness are out of the trusted base; only the checker and the CNF file
are in it, and the CNF file is small enough to read. The Theorem 1 formula and
three Theorem 2 formulas were also given LRAT proofs and accepted by the
independent `lrat-check` [@cruz-filipe-et-al-2017-efficient-certified-rat-verification], so no single checker carries the
result.

A certificate that cannot fail certifies nothing, so four negative controls drop
one hypothesis each and must return satisfiable with a witness that the
simulation and the C flip kernel independently confirm to be a flippable ice
state. Dropping $\mathbf{W} = 0$ from the Theorem 1 formula yields a state with
four flippable hexagons and winding $(0,0,-2)$, which shows the refutation is
about the flux constraint rather than an artefact of the encoding. Dropping the
frozen clauses at $(1,1,2)$ yields a zero-flux state with eight flippable
hexagons. Dropping one of the two half-diagonals yields 32 flippable hexagons at
$(2,2,2)$ and 60 at $(3,3,3)$, which is where those controls had to be placed,
since at a cell of transverse extent one the dropped translation is redundant and
the control would have been vacuous.

## Certified counting

A count has no short proof of the DRAT kind, so counting takes a different route,
certified knowledge compilation [@bryant-et-al-2025-certified-knowledge-compilation]. The formula is compiled to a
decision-DNNF representation by `d4` [@lagniez-marquis-2017-improved-decision-dnnf-compiler]; `cpog-gen` turns that
representation into a CPOG proof object asserting both that the representation
implies the formula and that the formula implies the representation; and
`cpog-check` verifies the proof object against the original CNF and prints its
own model count. Only `cpog-check`'s number is treated as certified, and the
compiler's raw number is recorded separately and required to agree.

Nineteen of twenty formulas were certified, the exception a compiler timeout at
$(3,3,3)$ rather than a disagreement. The certified counts are 90, 12 and 12 at
$(1,1,1)$ for ice states, zero-flux states and frozen zero-flux states; 3,618,
388 and 68 at $(1,1,2)$; 181,122, 15,024 and 528 at $(1,1,3)$; 204 for the frozen
zero-flux states of $(1,2,2)$; and, at two cells the project's 64-bit enumerator
cannot reach, **612** frozen zero-flux states at $(2,2,2)$ and **968** at
$(1,2,3)$. Seven further certified counts at $(1,1,1)$ underwrite an unrelated
three-bond obstruction. Every count already known by two other methods came back
equal to it.

One caveat is structural. A model count runs over all variables of the formula,
auxiliaries included, so a certified count is a count of the encoding and agrees
with the count of ice states only if every auxiliary is functionally determined by
the bond variables. That it is, for the cardinality encoding used here, is checked
rather than assumed: the ten rows whose certified count equals a count known
independently span three constraint sets and four cells, and the 612 and 968 use
the same encoding shape and are each equal to a projected enumeration whose
blocking clauses were placed on the bond variables alone. Every state of both
censuses was also re-verified individually by the ice-rule and winding predicates
and by the C flip kernel. The CPOG proof objects run to hundreds of megabytes
each and are not kept in the repository, so re-checking a count means
recompiling; the DRAT and LRAT proofs are all under 25 kB and are committed.

## Re-checking from the repository

From the repository root, with `elan` on the path:

```sh
cd formal/theorem1 && lake build && lake env lean Axioms.lean
cd formal/theorem2 && lake build && lake env lean Check.lean && lake exe hexexport export
python3 -m pytest tests/test_lean_bridge.py tests/test_frozen.py tests/test_cnf.py
```

Each `lake build` must end with `Build completed successfully` and no warnings,
and the two audits must show only `propext` and `Quot.sound`. The unsatisfiability
certificates need no project code: in
`experiments/07_certificates/results/certificates/` each claim has a `.cnf`, a
`.drat` and the checker's recorded output, so `drat-trim thm1_111_drat.cnf
thm1_111_drat.drat` reproduces the Theorem 1 verdict, and likewise for the fifteen
`thm2_<axis>_<cell>_drat` triples and the four LRAT pairs. Regenerating everything
including the counts is `run_certificates.py --items A,B,C,D`, which needs
`cadical`, `drat-trim`, `lrat-check`, `d4`, `cpog-gen` and `cpog-check` on the
path.

# Related work

Configurations with no flippable plaquette are a familiar feature of the dimer
models pyrochlore ice sits alongside; in quantum dimer models they are the
staggered configurations at the extreme of the flux range, isolated under local
plaquette dynamics and hence exact eigenstates at the Rokhsar--Kivelson point
[@rokhsar-kivelson-1988-superconductivity-quantum-hard-core-dimer-gas; @moessner-raman-2010-quantum-dimer-models]. The closest three-dimensional bipartite case, the
cubic-lattice dimer model, has been studied by Monte Carlo in its Coulomb phase
[@huse-et-al-2003-coulomb-liquid-dimer-models-3d] and its Rokhsar--Kivelson relaxation examined at a single size
[@lauchli-et-al-2008-dynamical-dimer-correlations-at-rk-points]; the corresponding exponent was measured on the square lattice
[@isakov-et-al-2011-dynamics-conformal-quantum-critical-points]. For spin ice the practical response to local dynamics that moves
between ice states too slowly has been to replace it, the loop algorithm of
Barkema and Newman [@barkema-newman-1998-monte-carlo-ice-models] being adapted to the pyrochlore lattice by
Melko and Gingras [@melko-gingras-2004-dipolar-spin-ice-monte-carlo] with cluster variants following [@otsuka-2014-cluster-algorithm-spin-ice;
@shinaoka-2011-pyrochlore-heisenberg-spin-ice-loop-algorithm]. The present work runs the local chain deliberately and asks what
its frozen set is, which is the question those algorithms were designed to make
irrelevant.

The link to the quantum problem is the Rokhsar--Kivelson correspondence
[@rokhsar-kivelson-1988-superconductivity-quantum-hard-core-dimer-gas; @henley-2004-classical-to-quantum-dynamics-at-rohsar-kivelson-points], sharpened by Castelnovo and co-workers into the
observation that a Hamiltonian at such a point admits a stochastic-matrix-form
decomposition, so that its ground-state phase diagram is an equilibrium partition
function and its excitation spectrum a set of classical relaxation rates
[@castelnovo-et-al-2005-generalized-rokhsar-kivelson-stochastic-matrix]. Under that correspondence the generator of the hexagon-flip
chain is the ring-exchange Hamiltonian of pyrochlore spin ice at its
Rokhsar--Kivelson point [@hermele-et-al-2004-pyrochlore-photons-u1-spin-liquid; @shannon-et-al-2012-quantum-ice-monte-carlo], so Theorem 2's
$3\binom{2L}{L}^{2}$ is a lower bound on the ground-space degeneracy at zero
flux. An exponentially large set of states inert under a local
constraint-preserving dynamics is what the recent literature calls Hilbert space
fragmentation, or shattering, a frozen state in the present sense being a
one-dimensional Krylov sector [@sala-et-al-2020-hilbert-space-fragmentation-dipole-conserving; @khemani-et-al-2020-hilbert-space-shattering-localization]; there the mechanism is
usually a set of local constraints or a conserved dipole moment, where here it is
the ice rule together with the geometry of the hexagon, explicit enough to be
counted exactly. The consequence for sampling is a bottleneck of the sort turned
into slow-mixing theorems elsewhere, as for Glauber dynamics of the six-vertex
model in its ordered phases [@fahrbach-randall-2019-slow-mixing-glauber-dynamics-six-vertex-model], although the states counted here are
isolated points rather than a barrier between two large regions, so what they
support is disconnection rather than slow mixing.
