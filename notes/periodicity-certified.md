# The model and the question

Pyrochlore ice is the six-vertex constraint on the pyrochlore lattice. Take
the diamond lattice, put a spin on every bond, and read the spin as an arrow
along that bond. The pyrochlore sites are the bonds, and the tetrahedra are
the diamond vertices. The ice rule asks that exactly two of the four arrows
at every vertex point in. Positions are integers in units of $a/4$, a
quarter of the conventional cubic edge, so a conventional cell is four units
on a side and an $(a,b,c)$ block of cells is a torus with box $(4a, 4b, 4c)$
holding $8abc$ vertices and $16abc$ spins. The physics behind the model is
standard [@bramwell-gingras-2001-spin-ice-state; @castelnovo-et-al-2008-magnetic-monopoles-spin-ice], and what follows is a finite,
combinatorial question about it.

The elementary move on the ice manifold reverses the arrows around a
hexagonal plaquette of the diamond lattice, which is legal exactly when the
six arrows already circulate head to tail. A hexagon on which they do is
*flippable*, and a state with no flippable hexagon is **frozen**: an
isolated point of the flip dynamics, and in the Rokhsar–Kivelson reading an
exact zero-energy eigenstate that is not the uniform superposition
[@rokhsar-kivelson-1988-superconductivity-quantum-hard-core-dimer-gas]. Hexagons are derived rather than tabulated, as closed
non-backtracking six-walks of zero unwrapped displacement, because the
quotient graph of a small torus also contains six-cycles that wind and whose
reversal would change the flux sector.

Flux is the net arrow current through the three coordinate cut planes,
written $W = (W_x, W_y, W_z)$, and it is conserved by hexagon flips. This
note is about the zero-flux sector alone.

The torus carries an abelian group of FCC translations, generated by
$(0,2,2)$, $(2,0,2)$ and $(2,2,0)$ modulo the box, of order $4abc$: 32
elements at $L = 2$, 108 at $L = 3$, 256 at $L = 4$. Each translation
permutes the bonds, hence acts on states. The **stabiliser** of a state is
the subgroup fixing it, and a state whose stabiliser is trivial is
**aperiodic**. The question is whether a frozen zero-flux state of a cubic
cell can be aperiodic.

At the thin cells the answer is plainly yes, and often. At the first cubic
cell it is no, and the census below says so exhaustively. The result of this
note is that it is also no at the second cubic cell, where no enumeration is
possible, and that the same computation at the third cubic cell did not
finish. Why the phenomenon should hold at every $L$ is a separate question,
partly answered by an analytic reduction of the conjecture to a statement
about dipole types that is not about cubic cells at all. That work is
recorded in `docs/frozen-structure-2026-09-05.md` and is not covered here.

# The encoding

One CNF encoding serves every claim in this note, and its variables are the
arrow bits themselves: bond $e$ is DIMACS variable $e + 1$, true when the
arrow points from the A-sublattice end of the bond to the B end. Auxiliary
variables are allocated above the bond variables and are never projected on,
so a model restricted to the bond variables is a state and nothing else.

**Ice rule.** At either sublattice the condition "exactly two of the four
incident arrows point in" reduces to "exactly two of the four incident bits
are set", and exactly-two-of-four over four literals is eight clauses with
no auxiliaries: forbid any three true, forbid any three false.

**Zero flux.** Because flux is conserved, one cut plane per axis is enough.
The plane $x_j = 1/2$ is a good choice, since A-sites have even coordinates,
so the only bond segments crossing it leave an A-site at $x_j = 0$ with
$\mathrm{sgn}(d_j) = +1$. There are exactly $4abc/\text{cells}_j$ of them
and every one carries $+1$, so $W_j = 2\,\mathrm{popcount} - n_{\text{cross}}$
and the constraint $W_j = 0$ is the single cardinality condition that
exactly half of the crossing bits are set, encoded with a sequential
counter. That this cut-plane flux agrees bit for bit with the project's own
winding function is checked over every ice state of three cells rather than
assumed.

**Frozen.** Walking a hexagon in cycle order, the traversal alternates with
respect to each bond's own A-to-B orientation, so flippability is the
statement that the six bits alternate: positions 0, 2, 4 all equal to $b$
and positions 1, 3, 5 all equal to $1 - b$. Blocking both patterns is two
six-clauses per hexagon, with no auxiliaries at all, which is why the frozen
formula stays small and prunes hard. The encoding was cross-checked against
a reference implementation written directly from the definition, over every
hexagon of three cells, with no mismatches.

**Aperiodicity.** A state is aperiodic exactly when, for every nontrivial
translation $t$, some bond disagrees with its image. That is a disjunction
of disequalities, so it needs one auxiliary per moved bond per translation:
$d_{t,e}$ with the two clauses $(\neg d \vee x_e \vee x_f)$ and
$(\neg d \vee \neg x_e \vee \neg x_f)$, where $f = \pi_t(e)$, together with
one long clause $(d_{t,e_1} \vee \cdots \vee d_{t,e_m})$ per translation.
The definition is one-directional because only one witness per translation
is wanted, which halves the clause count, and bonds fixed by $t$ are skipped
because their auxiliary could never satisfy the disjunction.

This block dominates the formula. At $L = 3$ it is 92,555 of the 99,035
clauses, and it is what makes the instance hard: the base formula
$\text{ice} \wedge W = 0 \wedge \text{frozen}$ at $L = 3$ is satisfiable in
1.81 s, whereas requiring 107 simultaneous disagreements is not settled in
two hours.

**The Cauchy reduction.** The stabiliser is a subgroup of a finite abelian
group, so if it is nontrivial it contains an element of prime order.
Excluding invariance under the prime-order translations alone is therefore
equivalent to excluding it under all of them, and the two encodings have the
same models rather than merely the same satisfiability. The saving depends
on the group. At $L = 3$ the group of order 108 has 3 elements of order 2,
26 of order 3 and 78 of order 6, so 29 of the 107 nontrivial translations
are of prime order and the formula falls from 48,600 variables and 99,035
clauses to 14,904 and 31,565. At $L = 4$ the group of order 256 is a
2-group, only its seven involutions have prime order, and the reduction is
larger still: 268,288 variables and 540,927 clauses become 14,336 and
32,775.

The reduction is gated rather than trusted. At $(1,1,2)$, $(1,2,2)$ and
$(1,2,3)$ the models of the full and reduced encodings were enumerated
separately and compared as sets, not as counts, and both were compared with
a third, solver-free method: a stabiliser census of the whole frozen
zero-flux set. All three agree, at 16, 64 and 336 aperiodic states
respectively. A further test checks the group-theoretic step state by state,
with no solver at all, over each of the 968 frozen zero-flux states of
$(1,2,3)$.

# The first cubic cell

At $L = 2$ the cell has 128 spins and 128 hexagons, beyond the project's
64-bit enumerator, but the frozen constraint prunes the SAT search so
sharply that the whole set can be listed. Blocking on bond variables only,
the search exhausts in 4.2 s and returns **612 frozen zero-flux states**
from a formula of 512 variables and 1,536 clauses. Every one of the 612 was
then re-verified independently by the project's ice-rule and winding
predicates and by the C flip kernel, with no failures.

Two methods agree on the number, since the project's own depth-first
enumerator cannot reach this cell. The SAT enumeration gives 612, and a
certified model count gives 612, obtained by compiling the formula with `d4`
and checking the compilation with `cpog-check`, whose own line
`Regular model count = 612` is what is treated as certified. The same
pipeline certifies 968 at $(1,2,3)$ and reproduces the smaller counts that
the enumerator and a frontier dynamic program already knew: 90, 3,618 and
181,122 ice states, 12, 388 and 15,024 zero-flux states, and 12, 68, 528 and
204 frozen states across four cells. So the encoding is known to agree with
the enumerator wherever both can run, before the counts it alone can reach
are believed.

The census answers the question directly. Under the 32 FCC translations the
stabiliser orders of the 612 states are distributed as follows.

| stabiliser order | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| states | 192 | 288 | 120 | 12 |

The minimum is 2. **No frozen zero-flux state of $(2,2,2)$ is aperiodic.**

The same statement was then put to a solver as an existence query, so that
it carries a proof object rather than an enumeration. The formula
$\text{ice} \wedge W = 0 \wedge \text{frozen} \wedge \text{aperiodic}$ at
$(2,2,2)$ has 4,480 variables and 9,503 clauses, CaDiCaL refutes it in
3.08 s, and the 5,278,492-byte DRAT proof is accepted by `drat-trim` in
2.24 s. The prime-order form of the same query is smaller (1,408 variables,
3,335 clauses), is refuted in 1.482 s, and its 5,245,914-byte proof is
accepted in 1.073 s. An in-process solver agrees with both. The negative
control drops only the frozen clauses and becomes satisfiable, and its
witness is an aperiodic zero-flux ice state with 22 flippable hexagons, so
the certificate is capable of failing.

The breakdown matters downstream. Of the 612, 108 belong to the
translation-invariant family that the project's second frozen-state theorem
constructs and 420 are tilings of frozen states of a proper sub-cell, the
family sitting inside the tilings, which leaves **192 in neither class**,
every one of stabiliser order exactly 4. Read through the dipole
classification of the companion note the same 612 split as 312 states in
which all three dipole axes occur, all of them carrying a whole-cube
coordinate period, and 300 with an axis missing, of which 108 have such a
period and 192 do not. Both routes count 420 states with a whole-cube
period, as they must, since that set is the tilings by definition.

# The second cubic cell

## Why direct solving failed

At $L = 3$ the cell has 432 spins, 432 hexagons and a translation group of
order 108. Enumeration is out of the question — the blocking-clause search
was abandoned after twenty minutes without reaching a 3,000-model cap, and
`d4` hit a 900 s wall compiling the frozen formula, so the number of frozen
zero-flux states at $L = 3$ is still unknown. The existence query is a
different matter, and it is the one that has to be asked.

Plain CaDiCaL [@biere-et-al-2020-sat-competition-2020-solvers-cadical-kissat-paracooba-plingeling] on the full 107-translation formula
(48,600 variables, 99,035 clauses) reached its two-hour cap with no verdict,
having logged 12,156,776
conflicts and 386,339 restarts while leaving 47,106 of 48,600 variables
unassigned at the root. That last figure is the informative one, because
after twelve million conflicts the search had fixed almost nothing at level
zero. On the reduced formula, given the same two hours each, CaDiCaL and Kissat
both timed out as well, writing 3.88 GB and 2.88 GB of partial proof that
certify nothing. A 3.26-fold reduction in formula size does not put this
instance inside a single-core budget.

Two facts locate the difficulty. The base formula without the aperiodicity
block is satisfiable in 1.81 s. The weaker query that excludes only the 26
whole-cell shifts, asking for a frozen zero-flux state that tiles no proper
sub-torus, is satisfiable in 15.29 s, and its model is a genuine frozen
state whose entire symmetry is the single half-box diagonal $(0,6,6)$. So
the hardness is created by the simultaneous exclusion of every translation
at once, and by nothing else.

## The scheme

Cube and conquer splits an instance into many easier ones by a lookahead
cuber and refutes each with a CDCL solver [@heule-et-al-2012-cube-and-conquer]. Here `march_cu`
at depth 12 produced 3,777 cubes in 112.7 s, of which 117 were already
refuted during cubing. Those cubes were sharded eight at a time across
twelve workers with a one-hour cap per shard: 468 shards came back UNSAT,
five hit the cap, none was satisfiable, and the refuted shards consumed
42,769 core-seconds (mean 91.4 s, maximum 2,450.9 s) in 7,138 s of wall
clock.

The five unfinished shards hold forty cubes, and they turned out not to be
hard at all. Re-solved with the cube's literals written into the formula as
unit clauses rather than passed through the incremental assumption
interface, all forty were refuted with a mean of 48.8 s and a maximum of
276.9 s. Preprocessing is the whole difference, because probing,
elimination and vivification act on unit clauses and not on assumptions. Those forty cubes
were nonetheless split further, by `march_cu` at depth 8, into 5,485
sub-cubes, so that the leaf set handed to the certifying pass was uniformly
easy. The final leaf set is therefore
$3{,}777 - 40 + 5{,}485 = 9{,}222$ cubes, and it partitions the search space
exactly as the original cube set did.

That settled the answer. Certifying it is a separate problem, and the
obvious route fails. Composing one DRAT proof of the whole refutation
requires a serial incremental pass, because it is the sequence of learned
clauses that makes the concatenation a proof. A four-hour attempt reached
11.9 GB without finishing, on the way to an object that backward DRAT
checking could not have processed in any reasonable time.

The route that works replaces one monolith by 9,222 independent objects.
Write $F$ for the reduced formula and $c_1, \dots, c_{9222}$ for the leaf
cubes. The certificate has two halves.

(A) *Every leaf is refuted.* For each $c_i$, the CNF $F$ with the cube's
literals appended as unit clauses is unsatisfiable, with a standalone LRAT
proof that `lrat-check` accepts on its own. No proof depends on any other,
there is no shared solver state and no ordering.

(B) *The leaves are exhaustive.* The CNF whose 9,222 clauses are the
negations of the leaf cubes is unsatisfiable, with a DRAT proof that
`drat-trim` accepts. This says more than is needed, since it rules out any
assignment escaping every leaf, not merely any model of $F$.

Together they force $F$ unsatisfiable. Suppose $M$ satisfied $F$. By (B),
$M$ satisfies some $c_i$; then $M$ satisfies $F \wedge c_i$, which (A)
refutes.

Two details are where such schemes usually go wrong. The cover proof is
taken over the negated cubes alone, with no reference to $F$. Had the cuber
dropped leaves it refuted during cubing, that CNF would have come back
satisfiable and the fallback would have been the weaker
$F \wedge \neg c_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \neg c_{9222}$. It did not, and
`drat-trim` reports 9,222 of 9,222 clauses in the core, so every leaf is
used and none is missing. And each leaf proof is checked against the leaf
CNF regenerated deterministically from $F$ and the cube, rather than against
a stored copy, so the binding between cube and proof is real.

## The run

| | |
|---|---|
| formula $F$ | 14,904 variables, 31,565 clauses, 595,623 B |
| leaves | 9,222 |
| solver verdicts | UNSAT 9,222, SAT 0, TIMEOUT 0 |
| checker verdicts | VERIFIED 9,222, FAILED 0 |
| solver core-seconds | 10,509.8 (mean 1.14, max 101.49) |
| checker core-seconds | 2,030.4 (mean 0.22, max 5.70) |
| wall clock | 1,139.1 s on 12 workers |
| proof bytes written | 18,569,991,562 (mean 2.0 MB, max 267.5 MB) |
| proofs retained at the 5 MB threshold | 8,588 (6.67 GB); 634 deleted |

A pre-flight on 24 leaves drawn uniformly at random projected 0.12 h of wall
clock, low by a factor of about 2.5 on solver time, because the per-leaf
cost is heavy tailed and 24 samples do not see the tail. Checking was cheap
throughout: `lrat-check` ran at 19 % of the solving time, and even the
267 MB worst case took 5.7 s to check against 101 s to solve.

The cover proof is cheaper still. Its CNF is 9,222 clauses over 14,904
variables in 593,545 B, CaDiCaL refutes it in 0.078 s, the DRAT proof is
3,449,033 B, and `drat-trim` accepts it in 0.165 s with all 9,222 clauses in
the core. Both files are small enough to live in the repository, so that
half of the certificate needs no external storage.

The result:

> **No frozen zero-flux ice state of the cubic cell $(3,3,3)$ has trivial
> stabiliser under the FCC translation group of the torus.** Equivalently,
> every frozen zero-flux state of $(3,3,3)$ is invariant under some nonzero
> FCC translation.

## The manifest, the checker, and how to re-verify

`results/aperiodic3/manifest.json` (5.4 MB, schema
`ice-mixing/aperiodic3-cnc-certificate/1`) states the claim and the scheme
in prose and then records the formula's path, sha256 and size; the reduction
parameters, including the 29 of 108 translations used; the leaves file with
its sha256, count and provenance; the retention policy; tool versions and
source commits, namely CaDiCaL 3.0.1 at `c6073042` and
`drat-trim`/`lrat-check` at `2e3b2dc0`; the aggregate timings; the
cover-proof hashes and verdict; and one record per leaf, carrying its cube,
both verdicts, both timings, the proof's format, size and sha256, and, where
retained, its path.

`experiments/07_certificates/verify_cnc_certificate.py` re-checks all of it
and imports nothing outside the Python standard library, so it can be handed
to someone who has neither the project nor its dependencies. Given
`cadical`, `lrat-check` and `drat-trim` on the path and the proof directory
in place,

```
python3 experiments/07_certificates/verify_cnc_certificate.py
```

hashes the formula and the leaves against the manifest, rebuilds the cover
CNF from the leaves and re-checks its DRAT proof, then re-checks all 9,222
leaf proofs, taking retained proofs as stored and regenerating deleted ones
first. A regenerated proof is a fresh object whose hash need not match the
recorded one, and the verifier says so when it takes that path. A hash
mismatch on a *retained* proof means tampering, and the verifier fails on
it. The full pass reports `PASS` in 832.1 s on twelve workers, with 8,588
proofs checked as stored and 634 regenerated. Cheaper variants exist:
`--sample 20 --kept-only` is about a second, and `--sample 300` exercises
the regeneration path in about a minute.

The verifier can fail, which was established rather than assumed. Checking
leaf $i$'s proof against leaf $j$'s CNF gives `c NOT VERIFIED`; the same
proof against its own CNF gives `c VERIFIED`; a deliberately wrong sha256 in
the manifest is rejected before any checking; and the cover CNF with a
single leaf's clause dropped comes back satisfiable, so exhaustiveness is a
real constraint and not a tautology of the encoding.

Two limits should be stated. The certificate covers the prime-order reduced
formula, and the equivalence of that formula with the full 107-translation
encoding is established by tests and by the two-method gate rather than by a
proof object. Every leaf proof was produced by CaDiCaL and checked by
`lrat-check`, and only the cover proof uses the second checker, so there is no
cross-implementation confidence at the leaf level, and no formally verified
checker was used at all.

# The third cubic cell, and what it does not establish

At $L = 4$ the cell has 1,024 spins and the translation group has order 256.
Being a 2-group it has only seven prime-order elements, the nonzero half-box
shifts, so the Cauchy reduction is 18.7-fold on variables and the reduced
formula is *smaller* than the one at $L = 3$: 14,336 variables and 32,775
clauses against 14,904 and 31,565. On formula size alone $L = 4$ looks
cheaper than $L = 3$.

It is not. Both CaDiCaL and Kissat time out on the reduced formula at a
15-minute cap. `march_cu` aborts on it at every depth and under every option
combination tried, with an assertion failure in its bookkeeping of big
clauses that have shrunk to binary. The seven aperiodicity clauses of length
1,024 are what trips it, and a ternary ladder over fresh variables, used for
cubing only, works around it. Nothing downstream trusts the cuber: the cubes
mention only original variables, so each leaf is solved against the original
formula, and the exhaustiveness half of the certificate checks the cube set
on its own.

Cubing at depth 20 gave 18,149 cubes, 7,473 of them refuted during cubing.
Of the 18,149, some 18,115 were refuted with an `lrat-check`-verified proof
inside a 900 s cap and 34 were not, in 5,565 s of wall clock on twelve
workers. Splitting those 34 by `march_cu` at depth 8 produced 5,038
sub-cubes, of which 4,631 were refuted and **407 were not**. The final leaf
set is $18{,}149 - 34 + 5{,}038 = 23{,}153$ cubes, with 22,746 refuted under
a checked proof against 407 unrefuted. The cover proof over that leaf set
verifies: CaDiCaL refutes it in 0.532 s, `drat-trim` accepts the
15,130,254-byte proof in 1.157 s, and all 23,153 clauses are in the core.
The manifest records `conclusion: INCOMPLETE`, marks its claim text
`PARTIAL`, and lists the 407 unrefuted leaves by index. The verifier passes
17,658 stored leaf proofs with none failing in 215.1 s, reporting
`PASS (partial run)`, and a further 120-leaf sample exercising the
regeneration path passes as well.

What that establishes is precise. The leaves partition the assignment space,
which the cover proof certifies and which summing $2^{-|c|}$ over them
confirms at exactly 1; the same sum over the 407 unrefuted leaves is
$1.516 \times 10^{-6}$. So no frozen aperiodic state exists anywhere in
99.99985 % of the search space, one part in 659,547 remaining, and **no leaf
anywhere at any level was satisfiable**. What it does not establish is the
theorem. One unrefuted leaf would be enough to leave the question open, and
there are 407.

The residue resists splitting, which is the substantive finding of the
stage. The 34 level-one cubes represented about 30,600 unresolved
core-seconds, their 5,038 children consumed roughly 146,000 core-seconds and
407 remain. A depth-6 split of those 407 produces 17,149 sub-leaves, of
which a 96-leaf uniform sample still had 11.5 % failing to close inside
300 s, projecting 767,006 solver core-seconds, or 18.2 h on twelve workers,
and it would leave a residue of its own. That level was not launched. Across
every level the run consumed 200,581 solver core-seconds and 9,214.9 checker
core-seconds and wrote 124.6 GB of proof, the largest single proof being
2.60 GB.

The 407 are a small, named object, descending from 29 of the 34 level-one
cubes with the worst single parent contributing 46, and they are isolated on
disk. Finishing by brute force is affordable and unattractive, since
splitting has so far increased total work rather than reduced it. A
different cuber, or a hand-chosen split on the bond variables of one
sub-cell rather than a lookahead choice, is the cheaper thing to try, as is
understanding what makes those cubes hard, which is where the structural
work of the companion note bears.

# The thin cells, for contrast

Aperiodic frozen zero-flux states are not rare in general. They are common
whenever some extent of the cell is 1, and they are absent from every cell
enumerated so far whose three extents are all at least 2.

| cell | spins | frozen $W = 0$ | aperiodic |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(1,1,2)$ | 32 | 68 | 16 |
| $(1,2,2)$ | 64 | 204 | 64 |
| $(1,2,3)$ | 96 | 968 | 336 |
| $(1,3,3)$ | 144 | 2,880 | 1,584 |
| $(2,2,2)$ | 128 | 612 | 0 |
| $(2,2,3)$ | 192 | 4,368 | 0 |

At $(1,3,3)$ more than half the frozen zero-flux states are aperiodic. The
contrast is sharp enough to say that the periodicity phenomenon is a fact
about cells thick in every direction rather than about cubic cells as such,
and the $(2,2,3)$ row, an exhaustive enumeration of 4,368 states, is the
cheapest evidence for that reading. It also rules out the lazy reading
under which aperiodic frozen states are merely too rare to find at larger
sizes. They exist in quantity, and only the thickness of the cell
suppresses them.

# Related work

Cube and conquer was introduced to make lookahead and CDCL solvers
cooperate on instances that neither settles alone, the cuber splitting the
problem into many cubes and the CDCL solver refuting each [@heule-et-al-2012-cube-and-conquer].
It is what carried the two best-known combinatorial refutations of the last
decade, the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem [@heule-et-al-2016-boolean-pythagorean-triples-cube-and-conquer] and
Schur number five [@heule-2018-schur-number-five]. In both the answer and the certificate
came out of the same machinery and the certificate was the harder half. The
general argument for that style of result, and for taking the proof object
as the unit of trust, is Heule and Kullmann's [@heule-kullmann-2017-science-of-brute-force]. Keller's
conjecture is the other standard example [@brakensiek-et-al-2020-keller-conjecture-resolution].

Clausal proof formats are what make such results checkable. DRAT is the
format solvers emit, and `drat-trim` checks it by backward resolution and
trims it to a core [@wetzler-et-al-2014-drat-trim]. Backward checking is expensive, often
as expensive as solving, and LRAT removes that cost by carrying antecedent
hints in the proof so that a checker verifies in one forward linear pass
[@cruz-filipe-et-al-2017-efficient-certified-rat-verification]. That difference is what the $L = 3$ certificate turns
on. Here 9,222 LRAT proofs check in 19 % of the time it took to find them,
where one composed DRAT proof of the same refutation was not checkable at
any size that could be produced. LRAT also makes a formally verified checker
reachable, `cake_lpr` being one verified in CakeML down to its machine code
[@tan-et-al-2021-verified-propagation-redundancy-checking]. Nothing here uses one, and that is the next rung. The
certified model count at $L = 2$ uses the CPOG toolchain, which produces a
checkable proof of a knowledge-compilation result and hence of a model count
[@bryant-et-al-2023-certified-knowledge-compilation].

On the physics side the ice-rule manifold has been studied since Pauling's
residual-entropy argument [@pauling-1935-ice-structure-entropy] and Anderson's identification of
the pyrochlore Ising ground-state condition with the ice rule
[@anderson-1956-ordering-antiferromagnetism-ferrites], with the two-dimensional case solved exactly by Lieb
[@lieb-1967-square-ice-residual-entropy] and the pyrochlore spin-ice realisation established
experimentally by Harris and co-workers [@harris-et-al-1997-geometrical-frustration-ho2ti2o7]. The manifold carries
an emergent gauge structure [@hermele-et-al-2004-pyrochlore-photons-u1-spin-liquid], and adding dipolar interactions
selects an ordered, periodic ground state out of it [@melko-et-al-2001-long-range-order-dipolar-spin-ice]. The
statement in this note is transverse to all of that. It concerns the states
the local dynamics cannot leave rather than the states it visits, and the
frozen set is where the standard height or flux description gives no
purchase, because these states are isolated points and not typical members
of a sector. The dimer literature has an analogous separation, its
variational and limit-shape theory describing typical tilings [@cohn-et-al-2001-variational-principle-domino-tilings;
@kenyon-et-al-2006-dimers-and-amoebae; @sheffield-2005-random-surfaces] while frozen or rigid configurations sit
outside it. I am not aware of a prior statement, for ice on the pyrochlore
lattice, that the frozen states of a periodic cell must themselves be
periodic, and what is offered here is finite evidence for one at the two
smallest cubic cells rather than a proof in general.
