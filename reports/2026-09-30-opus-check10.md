## Re-derivation with hypotheses

Notation: for axis $k$ and sublattice $S$, the $k$-lines of $S$ are indexed by the pairs
$(\alpha, \beta)$ of transverse coordinates of the parity of $S$. **Every such pair is a line**,
because for even $\alpha$ and $\beta$ the third coordinate is fixed modulo 4, and likewise for B.
So the line functions live on a full product $X_S \times Y_S$ on every cell. Theorem 5 and
Lemma 4 need this.

**Z-1.** $S$ two-way on $k$. $P = (h+s)/2$ and $Q = (h-s)/2$ count the two signs.
- $h$ is separable by Theorem 5, first statement. This holds in every frozen state.
- $s$ is separable by Theorem 5, second statement. It needs every hole of the $k$-hole-lines of
  $S'$ oriented; **U supplies this.**
- $PQ = 0$ by Theorem 4. It needs the holes of the line and of the Theorem 3 diamonds oriented;
  **U supplies this.** Theorem 3 holds at every separation on every torus, by the lift argument
  after Theorem 9. Theorem 4's bridgeheads lie on four distinct neighbouring lines, since
  $\alpha \pm 1$ differ by 2 modulo $4a \geq 4$.
- Lemma 4 needs $P$ and $Q$ non-negative and **neither identically zero**. Both signs occur on
  $S$ (two-way), so this is supplied.
- Conclusion: both $P$ and $Q$ are functions of one coordinate $x_n$, so $h$ is as well, and on
  each plane at most one of $P(c)$ and $Q(c)$ is positive.
- The normal is unique. If $P$ were also a function of the other coordinate it would be constant.
  With $PQ = 0$, one of $P$ and $Q$ would then vanish identically, which is excluded.

(a) Both theorems hold on every cell under "every hole oriented" as the note proves them, and the
non-vanishing condition of Lemma 4 is supplied. Answer: yes.

**Z-2**, with $k$ two-way globally and carried by both sublattices.
- *Case I* (both sublattices two-way). The normals are $n$ for A and $m$ for B; suppose
  $m \neq n$. Then $\{n, m\}$ are exactly the two transverse axes. A has a non-empty plane
  $x_n = c$ of sign $+$ (with $c$ even), and B has a non-empty plane $x_m = d$ of sign $-$ (with
  $d$ odd). Both are full by Z-1.
  - The pair $(c+1, d)$ is odd-odd, so it is a B-line, and it exists on every cell. Its
    transverse offset from the A-lines $(c, d \pm 1)$ is $(-1, \pm 1)$, so they are diagonal
    neighbours. Those A-lines lie in the full A-plane $c$.
  - Theorem 3 (U) then gives a contradiction. Only non-emptiness of the two planes is needed.
  - Adjacent planes: a B-line $(c \pm 1, \beta)$ neighbours the A-line $(c, \beta + 1)$, which is
    non-empty when the A-plane is.
- *Case II* (A two-way, B of one sign $\sigma$). A has a full plane $c$ of sign $-\sigma$. By
  Theorem 3, every B-line at $x_n = c \pm 1$ is empty.
  - $h^B$ is separable (Theorem 5, first statement), non-negative and not identically zero, with a
    zero. So its zero set is the product of the argmins, $I \times J$.
  - The zero set contains $\{c \pm 1\} \times Y_B$, so $J = Y_B$ and $G$ is constant.
  - So $J = Y_B$ follows, $h^B$ is a function of $x_n$, and its non-empty planes are full, with the
    same count on each line.
  - The sign statement is immediate, because B has one sign and the planes next to $-\sigma$
    planes of A are empty.
- *Case III* (A of sign $\sigma$, B of sign $-\sigma$). I re-derived Theorem 7's second case as
  written; it uses only Theorems 3 and 5.
  - The empty set of A is a non-empty product. If both factors were proper, a full column and a
    full row of A would empty the two neighbouring columns and rows of B. B's empty set would then
    be the whole product, but B carries $k$.
  - So the empty lines are complete planes of one normal, and $h^A$ is a function of it. The same
    holds for B.
  - The normal is common, by the Case I argument, which needs only that the two full planes carry
    opposite signs.
  - Full planes of A and B are never adjacent.
  - This is exactly what Z-2 uses, with the **same normal for both sublattices**. Oddness, the
    hypothesis of Theorem 7, is not used in this case.
- *Exhaustiveness.* Each sublattice carries $k$, so each is two-way or one-signed. Two one-signed
  sublattices of the same sign would make $k$ one-way, so the cases (TW,TW), (TW,OS) up to
  exchange, and (OS,OS) with opposite signs cover everything. Answer (b): the named lines are
  diagonal neighbours; the B-line exists for every choice; non-empty suffices; $J = Y_B$ follows;
  Theorem 7's second case gives a common normal; the cases are exhaustive.

**Z-3.** Theorem 15 holds in every solution of the type model on every cell, with the same $P$,
$Q$, $R$ in all three identities. $h_x/\ell_x = 1 + R(z) - Q(y)$ holds on all pairs $(y, z)$ of the
parity of $S$, and every such pair occurs (see Notation). So independence of $z$ forces $R$ to be
constant on the $z$-values of $S$, and likewise for the other axes. I checked all six (k, n)
combinations: $i(k)$ is the axis outside $\{k, n(k)\}$.
- The image of $i$ cannot be a single axis, because $i(k) \neq k$.
- If the image contains $a$ and $b$, the $h_c$ formula involves only the profiles of $a$ and $b$,
  so $h_c$ is constant.
- The normals are common to A and B by Z-2 (all three cases), so the image and $c$ are common.

Answer (c): yes on every cell; the non-constancy exclusion is correct; the common normal is
supplied by Z-2.

**Z-4.** The $k$-lines of both sublattices are the pairs $(\alpha, \beta)$ modulo $(4p, 4q)$ with
$\alpha \equiv \beta \pmod 2$. The steps $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ generate that index-2 lattice in
$\mathbb{Z}^2$, and hence its image on the torus. So the graph is connected for all extents,
including 1 and 2, where each line still has its four distinct neighbours. Theorem 3 applied twice,
through a vertex of a neighbouring line (every line is non-empty), gives one sign. Answer (d): yes.

**Z-5.** Suppose all three axes are two-way. Z-2 applies to each $k$, since both sublattices carry
$k$ by hypothesis. Z-3 then gives an axis $c$ with $h^A_c$ and $h^B_c$ constant, at least 1 because
each sublattice has a type-$c$ vertex on some line. Z-4 makes $c$ one-way, which is a
contradiction.

**Z-6.** In an odd frozen state with every hole oriented, if some type is absent its axis is empty.
Otherwise every type occurs somewhere.
- The contrapositive of $M'$ ("one sublattice lacks $k$ and the other has it" implies even curl)
  applies to an odd state, with its hypotheses exactly. So both sublattices carry all three types.
- Z-5 then gives an axis that is not two-way; it is non-empty, so it is one-way.

Answer (e): $M'$ is used within its hypotheses. At extents 1 and 2, see corrections 2 and 3. The
argument itself never forms a hexagon or chain that could wrap (hexagon vertices span at most 3 in
each coordinate and are distinct modulo 4). The Z(L) clause relies on Corollary Z of the signed
report, which I did not re-check; it is not needed for H.

**(f) Hidden uses.** There is no use of cubic geometry. The classification of normals into two
classes "under the symmetries of the cubic cell" is commentary, not proof. There is no use of
$K_u$, walls, Lemma 5, Theorem 8 or $W = 0$. Oddness is used only through U (as its hypothesis)
and $M'$. Theorem 15 and Theorem 5's first statement are type-model facts. Theorems 3 and 4 and
Theorem 5's second statement are where orientation enters, as the report's "Where U enters" says.
